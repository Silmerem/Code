Story of the day

Two tankers were reported struck in the Strait of Hormuz, and Donald Trump told American media he is close to deciding whether to “annihilate” his Iranian foes — “anything could happen”. The Revolutionary Guard said it hit a Togo-flagged vessel making an “illegal passage”; the UK Maritime Trade Operations desk logged the other as an unknown projectile. This is the same week in which Saudi Arabia moved 2.8 million barrels a day through that strait, against 700,000 in August, because its bypass pipeline is broken. The workaround holds, one analyst told reporters, “so long as Iran allows it to” — and the president is publicly weighing whether to widen the war instead.

Economy 2

A fourth central bank joins the week, and the money behind the AI build-out starts buying smaller.

  • Bank of Japan hikes rates to a 31-year high to battle inflation
    France 2418 Sep

    A quarter point to 1.25% on a 7–2 vote — a level Japan has not seen since 1995 — with the yen jumping more than 2% at one point, to 155.28 to the dollar. Kazuo Ueda’s reasoning is the same as everyone else’s this week: oil. Four major central banks met in eight days and three of them tightened.

  • Anthropic and OpenAI hunt for smaller data-centre deals in the race to deploy capacity
    CNBC18 Sep

    After a year of gigawatt announcements, both are now said to be chasing 20–30 megawatt sites. That is a macro story as much as a technology one: the corporate issuance funding these builds was named this week as one of the forces pushing long bond yields to 2007 levels, and buying smaller is what capital discipline looks like before anyone admits to it.

AI 2

Six days after calling for independent evaluators, the company that called for them has named its first one.

  • Anthropic’s first embedded evaluator is … Accenture?
    TechCrunch18 Sep

    Staff from Accenture’s Faculty arm will work inside Anthropic red-teaming models, running alignment assessments and testing safeguards — and the two companies expect to put at least $1bn into the arrangement over five years. The question in TechCrunch’s headline is the right one. An evaluator that is also a billion-dollar commercial partner is not independent in the sense the word was used last weekend, and the gap between the proposal and the first implementation of it is the story.

  • Anthropic is operating a lab that conducts biology experiments
    TechCrunch18 Sep

    A wet lab in the Bay Area where its models run physical experiments, confirmed after the company bought a stealth biotech, Coefficient Bio, in April. The stated focus is fundamental biology rather than drug discovery. It is worth setting beside the same company’s statement a week ago that it had blocked attempts to use its models for bioweapons research: it is now doing biology itself, and the public has the company’s word for both halves.

Both from one outlet, which is the per-section limit; The Register, Axios, Fortune, Euronews and CNBC filed nothing else dated 18 or 19 September that qualified. Disclosure, eighth day running and larger than usual today: Anthropic makes Claude, the model that assembles this brief, and appears in three of this edition’s eleven headlines — both of these and the data-centre story in Economy. All three are reported sceptically, and the first is the least comfortable item this brief has carried about its own supplier. That is the correct direction for the discomfort to run.

UK Politics 1

The first foreign leader received at the prime minister’s Manchester office was the Taoiseach.

  • Burnham vows to deepen ties with the EU as he hosts Ireland’s Martin at ‘No 10 North’
    ITV News18 Sep

    Micheál Martin became the first head of government to visit the Manchester site, which is the point being made: a prime minister elected on devolution receiving foreign leaders outside London. Iran War 4

Two ships hit, a president musing about annihilation, and an oil market that has decided none of it matters this week.

  • Trump weighs a ‘big decision’ on Iran as tankers are hit in Hormuz
    Al Jazeera18 SepLive blog

    A second tanker was reported struck during the day. Away from the water, tens of thousands marched in Sanaa in support of the Houthis and against Saudi Arabia, while Yemen’s recognised government claimed thirty Houthi fighters killed in Taiz. The claims on both sides are the parties’ own.

  • Oil finishes the week flat as the market treats the Saudi pipeline outage as less disruptive than feared
    CNBC18 Sep

    Satellite tracking puts 2.8 million barrels a day through Hormuz over six days against 700,000 in August — the kingdom pushing its exports back through the strait it spent the summer avoiding. Both benchmarks stayed above $100. A flat week is not calm; it is the market concluding the substitution works, on an assumption about Iranian restraint that Iran has not offered.

  • What to know after a week of Houthi attacks that threaten Saudi oil
    NPR18 Sep

    The week assembled in one place, which is useful after five days of incremental reporting. The through-line is that Saudi Arabia is now a belligerent rather than a bystander, and that its energy infrastructure is reachable from two directions at once.

  • The cost of Iran’s regional leverage
    Al Jazeera18 SepOpinion

    An argued piece, flagged as opinion. Its case is that the chokepoint strategy buys Tehran bargaining power at a price paid in isolation from the Gulf states it needs, and it is included because that argument is not otherwise represented in this edition’s reporting.
