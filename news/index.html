Story of the day

Nvidia’s board voted unanimously yesterday to reinstate Jensen Huang as chief executive, eleven days after he resigned, and the shares rose about 31%. The market went with them: the S&P 500 gained 4.73% to 7,237.17 and the Nasdaq 6.51% to 24,089.56, the best day for both since April 2025. Six days ago this page reported the board chairman, Harvey Jones, telling an earnings call that the company was not interested in bringing him back, and reported the shares falling on it. That was accurate then and the board has now reversed it completely. The board says it consulted senior US government officials and concluded the reinstatement poses no national security risk; President Trump says he sealed it personally — “I made the deal”. The investigations that produced the resignation are all still open.

What the market just told you

On Friday this brief carried Fortune’s question — Nvidia can run without Jensen Huang, but can the AI boom? — and said nobody knew which way it would read. It has now been answered about as bluntly as markets ever answer anything. One man returning to one job moved the largest index on earth by nearly five per cent in a session. Whatever the AI trade is priced on, a meaningful part of it was priced on him. That is the market’s revealed judgement, not mine, and it is a statement about the fragility of the valuation rather than about the man.

AI 5

A unanimous board reversal, a president claiming credit, bipartisan fury in Congress, and every investigation still running.

  • Jensen Huang reinstated as Nvidia CEO; stock skyrockets 31%
    CNBC23 Nov

    The facts, in order. He resigned on 12 November after reports of a relationship with a woman US intelligence officials have alleged passes information to Beijing — an allegation Beijing calls groundless and which the Justice Department has not substantiated. The board has now returned him as president, chief executive and director, saying it spoke to senior government figures first and is confident there is no national security risk. The board’s own independent committee investigation continues regardless.

  • Nvidia board reinstates Jensen Huang as CEO
    Axios23 Nov

    The shortest account, and useful for the sequence it makes plain: the chairman told investors on the earnings call last week that the board did not intend to rehire him, and the board voted unanimously to rehire him five days later. Nothing published explains what changed in those five days except the share price and the intervention described below.

  • Nvidia brings Jensen Huang back, and Trump claims credit
    NPR23 Nov

    The president says he made the deal. Whether that is an accurate description of his role or a claim on someone else’s decision is not established, and this page is not in a position to settle it — but a head of government saying he personally arranged the chief executive of a $4 trillion company is a fact about how this was decided, and it sits oddly beside a board statement resting on its own consultation and judgement.

  • Congress shreds Nvidia’s ‘outrageous’ decision to bring back Huang
    CNBC23 Nov

    Objections from both parties: Representative Don Bacon, a Republican, calls it a failure of character; Senator Elizabeth Warren says the company still owes Congress answers on export-control compliance; the House minority leader says there will be an investigation, though his party does not take the chamber until January. Huang is due before the Senate Banking Committee on 3 December. The Securities and Exchange Commission has also opened a civil inquiry into whether the company’s disclosures misled investors, reported here via the Wall Street Journal — note that Friday’s version of the SEC story, via Reuters, described it as an inquiry into accounting practices. Those may be the same thing described twice or two different things; nobody has said which.

  • Incompetent or pragmatic? The board’s rehiring of Huang splits governance experts
    Fortune23 NovAnalysis

    The argument worth reading rather than the verdict. One side: a board that accepted a resignation, told investors it would not be reversed, and reversed it under share-price pressure has demonstrated it does not control the company. The other: a founder whose absence costs shareholders a quarter of their capital is a fact a board is obliged to act on. This brief takes no side; both cases are real and the first one is the one most of today’s coverage understates.

Iran War 2

Tehran calls the blockade military aggression and says Hormuz could reopen in two days — on its terms.

  • Iran says it will use ‘all available means’ to counter the US blockade
    Al Jazeera23 Nov

    The foreign ministry calls the naval blockade “military aggression”, a day after the US president threatened to sink Iranian tankers attempting to break it. Tehran repeats that the strait could reopen within two days if Washington halts hostilities, lifts the blockade and waives sanctions — the same conditions it has set for weeks, which is the point: nothing has moved except the number of ships on fire.

  • Iran vows ‘every means’ to counter the US blockade of Hormuz after Trump threat
    Euronews23 Nov

    The same statement from a second newsroom, carried deliberately: when a government’s words are the news, two independent renderings of them are worth more than one. Reported attacks on shipping in and around the strait are at their highest since the war began, with at least 25 tankers hit in the week to Sunday. Iran’s Revolutionary Guard claimed overnight that three more were set alight by naval mines; that claim is Iranian, unverified, and contradicted by an American statement that the main routes were cleared of mines.

Two headlines on one statement. The fullest Hormuz reporting is in continuously updated live pages, which this brief does not cite, and the strongest single piece on the attack data is CNN’s from Sunday, which falls outside today’s 24-hour window.
