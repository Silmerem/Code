Story of the day

The United States and Iran both say they control the Strait of Hormuz. Central Command’s position is that the strait “is controlled by the United States and our regional partners, not Iran”, and that four hundred ships and a hundred million barrels passed through in the last week; the Revolutionary Guards’ position is that any vessel transiting without their permission is unsafe, and they say a supertanker carrying more than two million barrels caught fire after striking a mine on Saturday. Both statements can be true at once, and that is the problem. A waterway where most traffic gets through and some of it burns is not controlled by anybody — it is a lottery with a ticket price, and the price now shows up in two of the world’s busiest trade routes at once. Brent touched $110 on Friday, the first time since May.

Iran War 5

Two powers claiming the same strait, and the Houthis opening a front inside Yemen’s own government-held capital.

  • US and Iran both claim control of the Strait of Hormuz amid attacks on vessels
    Al Jazeera17 Oct

    Central Command counts four hundred ships and a hundred million barrels through in a week and says the strait is not Iran’s to control; the Revolutionary Guards say vessels transiting without permission are unsafe and report a supertanker with more than two million barrels on fire after hitting a mine. Neither side is obviously lying. A strait can be mostly open and individually lethal at the same time, and insurers price the second.

  • How the US-Iran war upended two critical global trade routes
    CNN17 OctAnalysis

    Hormuz and Bab al-Mandeb mapped together, which is the right way to look at it: one strait is being contested by Iran and the United States and the other by the Houthis and the Saudi-led coalition, and a cargo leaving the Gulf for Europe has to clear both. There is no third route that does not add weeks.

  • Yemen’s Houthis claim responsibility for a fresh missile strike on Aden airport
    France 2417 Oct

    Missiles on the airport of the internationally recognised government’s own seat, with the group saying it hit a weapons depot and a military command headquarters, and no casualties reported. After a fortnight of strikes on Saudi territory this is the war turning back inward, onto the Yemeni government that started the ground offensive.

  • Houthis strike Yemen’s Aden: what that means
    Al Jazeera17 OctAnalysis

    The reading of the same strike: Aden is where the government, the central bank and the foreign missions sit, so hitting it is a message about the state rather than about the front line. Three weeks ago this was a frozen conflict over a coastline. It now has two capitals under fire.

  • Saudi-led coalition targets Houthi arms smuggling and destroys missile launch platforms
    Al Arabiya17 Oct

    Two ballistic missile launchers claimed destroyed east of Saada, alongside video of strikes on weapons said to be arriving by sea towards Hodeidah. These are the coalition’s own claims with the coalition’s own footage, and no independent party has verified them. The smuggling route is the part that matters: it is the supply line that determines how long the launcher count keeps needing to go up.
