Story of the day

An American was among the thirteen people killed at Riyadh airport on Saturday. Saudi authorities have now named the dead — four Saudis, two Bangladeshis, an American, an Egyptian, a Jordanian, a Syrian, a Palestinian and a Sudanese — and Rubio has confirmed the US citizen. That single fact is what has changed the American calculation: Trump, who said on Sunday only that the US “may” join the Saudi campaign, spoke to the crown prince on Monday and called the Houthis “misbehaving very badly”. A Houthi political bureau member has promised a “swift and painful” response against American interests across the region if Washington enters. Riyadh’s main airport has now been shut for four days, with missiles arriving on each of them. The question this brief asked on Sunday — whether the United States joins the Yemen war — is now being decided with an American death on the ledger.

Economy 4

The IMF says the world economy is holding up because of AI spending, and that the AI spending is part of the problem.

  • IMF warns the global economy is nearing the ‘limits of resilience’
    Axios13 OctToday

    Global inflation revised up to 4.7% for 2026 and 4.1% for 2027, on energy and food, with risks tilted to the downside. The phrase in the title is the fund’s own and it is doing deliberate work: an institution that spent two years describing a soft landing is now saying the absorbing capacity is close to spent.

  • IMF forecasts 3.1% global growth in 2026, with the eurozone at 1.1%
    Euronews13 OctToday

    The European numbers under the headline: 1.1% growth, inflation at 3.1% this year and 2.8% next. Set that beside last week’s French borrowing costs and Spain’s snap election and the eurozone is being asked to carry a debt repricing on one per cent of growth, which is the arithmetic behind every bond spread reported here this month.

  • IMF upgrades its 2026 growth forecast but warns of a ‘split’ outlook
    France 2413 OctToday

    The split is the useful idea: economies with AI investment and economies that merely import the energy inflation it causes are now on different paths. An upgrade that applies to some members and not others is a harder thing for a fund to manage than a downgrade that applies to all of them.

  • Global economy to slow less than expected in 2026 as the AI boom offsets the war hit: IMF
    CNBC12 Oct

    From the annual meetings in Bangkok, with the number that should worry people most: the fund now puts the probability of global inflation exceeding 5% in 2027 at about 35%, up from 30% in April. And it credits AI investment for the resilience while naming the same investment as a source of inflationary pressure and financial fragility — one boom doing both jobs.

The four accounts above frame the growth figure differently because they compare it with different baselines — April’s forecast, July’s, or last year’s outturn. This brief has not reconciled them and does not present any single one as the change; the inflation numbers, on which all four agree, are the firmer part of the release.

AI 4

After a fortnight of labs reporting their own failures, the question has become whether self-reporting is a system at all.

  • Can we trust OpenAI and Anthropic to monitor their own technology?
    Fortune12 OctAnalysis

    The question the last two weeks have forced, asked directly. The honest answer visible from the evidence is partial: both labs did disclose incidents nobody else would have found, and both took weeks to do it, and in Anthropic’s case a police force learned of a submission to its own tip line nine days after the company knew. Disclosure that arrives on the discloser’s timetable is better than silence and is not oversight. See the note below.

  • The White House wants AI companies to police themselves. OpenAI just showed why that’s dangerous
    Fortune12 OctAnalysis

    The argument: a firm that polices its own safety staff more vigorously than its own conduct has demonstrated what voluntary self-regulation produces. OpenAI says the three researchers it removed mishandled sensitive information outside company procedures; the researchers say they were raising safety concerns. Both can be true, and the point of an external regulator is that it does not have to take either side’s word.

  • Microsoft president says the UK is best placed to drive AI regulation
    TechCrunch12 Oct

    Brad Smith argues Britain rather than Washington or Brussels should lead on global AI safety rules, and — the substantive part — that labs should need sign-off from outside evaluators before release. He calls the White House’s voluntary accord a good step and not enough. Coming from the president of the company that hosts much of this industry’s compute, the call for mandatory pre-release approval is more significant than the choice of jurisdiction.

  • Meta unveils new frontier-class AI models to compete with OpenAI and Anthropic
    Axios12 Oct

    Muse Wonder and Muse Wonder Full, claimed as state of the art on agentic, memory and coding work. The claims are the company’s own and unverified, as every such claim this month has been. Note the timing: a frontier release on the same day two other firms were being asked whether they can supervise what they have already shipped.

Iran War 4

A capital’s airport closed for four days, an American among the dead, and Tehran offering to mediate.

  • Iran war live: Trump says the Houthis are ‘misbehaving’ as Riyadh flights are halted
    Al Jazeera13 OctTodayLive blog

    This morning’s page: the president has spoken to the Saudi crown prince and hardened his language without committing to anything, and King Khalid remains shut. The Saudi energy minister promises an “equally serious” response. “Misbehaving very badly” is a notably small phrase for an attack that killed an American citizen, and the gap between the words and the stakes is itself the information.

  • Deadly Houthi attacks on Saudi Arabia raise the stakes for the US
    NPR12 Oct

    The reckoning with an American death. Saturday’s strike on the passenger terminal killed thirteen and wounded more than three hundred, and the named dead span nine nationalities — a casualty list that reads like an airport departures board, which is what a strike on a terminal produces. Washington, London, Madrid and Ottawa have all told their citizens not to use it.

  • Fresh Houthi strike hits Riyadh airport a day after the deadly attack
    France 2412 Oct

    Sunday’s follow-up strike killed one more person, a Pakistani resident, and wounded eleven, and Monday brought missiles at the capital for a third consecutive day. The pattern — hitting the same terminal repeatedly after it has been emptied — suggests the target is the airport’s usability rather than the people in it, which does not make the deaths incidental.

  • Iran urges Saudi Arabia to pursue diplomacy after the Houthis escalate
    Al Jazeera12 Oct

    Tehran’s foreign minister telephoned his Saudi counterpart to urge a political solution and offered to help open a Saudi-Yemeni dialogue. Analysts quoted in the same piece hold that the Houthi attacks are coordinated with Tehran. Both things being reported together is the point: the state offering to mediate is widely held to be supplying one side, and this is the oldest position in the war — influence without accountability.

The length of the airport closure is not settled. Saudi authorities say “until further notice”; a notice to airmen indicates at least 17 October; one account has it extended by twenty-four hours on Monday evening. No source reconciles them, so no reopening date is given above.
