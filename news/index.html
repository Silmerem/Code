Story of the day

Two numbers published yesterday are the same number. American wholesale prices rose 1.3% in a single month, the sharpest jump since March 2022, and 6.3% over the year, with wholesale petrol up 20.3%. And 4.147 million barrels of oil got through the Strait of Hormuz on Monday — under a quarter of the pre-war volume — with at least ten vessels attacked in or near the strait since Sunday and a Singapore-flagged container ship sunk. One of those is an economic statistic and the other is a shipping lane, and they are the same event measured twice. The retail figures make the point domestically: spending rose 0.8%, but only 0.2% once petrol stations are taken out. Americans are not buying more. They are paying more for the same tank.

Finance 2

A trillion dollars has come off the AI complex this week, and the reason is the cost of borrowing.

  • Nvidia, AMD and Dell sink in a global tech rout on AI funding worries
    CNBC15 Oct

    Twenty of the world’s most valuable AI and semiconductor stocks have lost more than a trillion dollars of value since Monday, and the worry is not demand but financing: the build-out is being paid for with borrowed money at rates that keep rising. Broadcom’s Hock Tan said this week that access to capital could become the biggest constraint on AI companies. That is the inflation story and the technology story turning out to be one story.

  • Dollar retreats from an 18-month high despite strong inflation data
    CNBC15 Oct

    Yields down and the dollar off its high on the same day wholesale prices surged, because Fed officials talked the October meeting down. Currency markets are trading the committee’s nerve rather than the data, which is a reasonable thing to trade when the data points one way and the speeches point the other.

Economy 3

Wholesale prices are now rising faster than at any time since 2022, and the pass-through has already started.

  • Wholesale inflation surged in September, keeping affordability in focus ahead of the midterms
    CNN15 Oct

    Producer prices up 1.3% on the month and 6.3% on the year, with core wholesale inflation — stripping out food and energy — running at 0.7% monthly and a 5% annual rate. Core at 5% is the figure to keep: it says the energy shock has finished travelling through fuel tanks and is now inside the price of everything else.

  • US producer prices see their biggest jump since 2022 in an added challenge to Trump
    France 2415 Oct

    The forecast miss is the part worth noting: 1.3% against 0.6% expected on the month, and 6.3% against 5.5% on the year, with wholesale petrol up 20.3%. Economists were not close. Retail sales excluding fuel rose 0.2%, which the wire reads as flat real consumer spending — households standing still while the till rings higher.

  • The too-hot-for-comfort economy
    Axios15 Oct

    The mechanism nobody wants confirmed: margins rose sharply in September, which means firms are passing costs on rather than absorbing them. Through most of this war businesses took the hit to protect volumes. That phase appears to be over, and it is the point at which a supply shock becomes ordinary inflation.

AI 3

The strongest set of numbers the industry has produced, published into the worst week its shares have had.

  • Anthropic revenue jumps 46% to $16.8bn as the AI firm eyes a record $2 trillion IPO valuation
    Euronews15 Oct

    The detail yesterday’s first reports lacked: 46% growth on the quarter, a listing now aimed at November rather than October so that it falls after the midterms, and annualised revenue expected to finish 2026 below $100 billion. Growth of 46% in a quarter is extraordinary and it is also slower than before, which at a $2 trillion asking price is the number that has to keep being beaten. See the disclosure below.

  • Anthropic makes an AI bull case with $16.8bn in sales and positive cash flow
    Axios15 Oct

    The measure that answers this week’s question directly: every dollar spent on compute returned $1.70 of revenue in the quarter, and the company says it was profitable on an adjusted basis for a second consecutive quarter. If that ratio holds across the industry the financing panic in Finance above is wrong. Gartner’s arithmetic on Wednesday said it does not hold across the industry.

  • Nvidia’s European spending spree is about much more than Europe
    Fortune15 Oct

    Read against the rout: a chipmaker investing in its customers’ capacity is also underwriting the demand for its own product, which is a structure that works beautifully while capital is cheap. Capital stopped being cheap about three weeks ago.

Iran War 3

The strait is down to a quarter of its traffic and ships are being sunk in it.

  • Cargo ships sunk in the Strait of Hormuz as Iran ramps up attacks in the run-up to the midterms
    CNN15 Oct

    At least ten vessels attacked in or near the strait since Sunday, two Singapore-flagged container ships hit and one of them sunk; the Revolutionary Guards’ navy says a container ship struck a mine in an unauthorised lane. Kpler counted 4.147 million barrels through on Monday against roughly seventeen million before the war. The headline says ships plural were sunk and the account below it describes one, so this brief reports the one it can see.

  • Iran war updates: ships in the Strait of Hormuz still face ‘indiscriminate attacks’, maritime monitor warns
    CBS News15 OctLive blog

    “Indiscriminate” is the operative word and it is the monitor’s, not this brief’s: vessels with no connection to any party are being hit, which is what ends commercial traffic through a waterway faster than any blockade. The Houthis have separately told shipping companies and the staff at Saudi oil facilities that they are now “legitimate targets”.

  • Fighting in Yemen intensifies: what’s the latest on the battlefield?
    Al Jazeera15 Oct

    The ground war behind the shipping war, with Riyadh’s main airport shut until at least tomorrow and the coalition still striking fuel and transport sites across western Yemen. Two conflicts are being fought over the same cargo: one for the Red Sea coast and one for the Gulf, and between them they carry most of the oil that reaches Europe.
